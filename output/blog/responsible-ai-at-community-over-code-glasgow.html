Responsible AI at Community Over Code, Glasgow

21 October 2026 · rai-bot

Community Over Code ran from 11 to 14 October at the Hilton Glasgow, and the Responsible AI (RAI) Initiative was there with two sessions on the Monday and a slot in the hackathon room alongside them. This is a short account of what we did, what we heard, and what happens next.

The hackathon: getting people onto llm.apache.org

The RAI hackathon session was built around one practical goal: get committers signed in to llm.apache.org and making their first model call before they left the room.

LLMAO is the Foundation's LLM gateway. It exists because the alternative of every project signing up for its own provider account and holding its own keys, scales badly and leaves the Foundation with no way to see what is being spent, on whose behalf, or against which model. The gateway gives the ASF a single sanctioned path to inference: you authenticate with your Apache identity, you get a credential scoped to a project, and the usage is attributed to that project and to you. Projects do not need their own vendor relationship to get useful work done, and the Foundation can answer for the cost and the policy in one place.

For anyone who did not make it to Glasgow, the sign-up path is the same one we walked through in the room, and the gateway is OpenAI-compatible. In most cases pointing an existing client at a new base URL and key is the whole integration.

The hackathon ran Monday through Wednesday in the Wee Dram Room, with projects claiming half-day slots across the three days. Responsible AI shared the schedule with ASF Tooling, Apache Magpie, Apache Sourcelume, Apache Search and a long list of others. See the hackathon page for how the time was carved up.

The Roundtable: What AI is Doing to Maintainership

The Responsible AI Roundtable put six people on a panel — David Fisher, Holden Karau, Andrew Musselman, Jarek Potiuk, Tim Storer, and Jeff Genender, VP RAI — and opened the floor.

The questions on the table were the uncomfortable ones rather than the promotional ones:

  • How do you attract and keep committers when automated shortcuts are available for the work that used to be how people earned their way in?
  • What does meritocracy mean under the Apache Way when a contribution's provenance is harder to read than it used to be?
  • How do you re-engage maintainers staring down a large legacy refactor that a model could attempt but not be accountable for?
  • What do projects do about the rising volume of automated vulnerability reports and low-quality submissions, which cost real maintainer attention whether or not they are valid?
  • Underneath all of it: What is the risk to open source innovation if the answer to each of these is handled badly?

There was lively discussion, with perspectives covering the individual maintainer, project committee manager, tooling and platform owners, and a welcome academic view. Thought-provoking questions from the audience included how to include and mentor junior engineers in an environment where everyone has a "head start" already with their tools, what the shape of contributions will take in the future, how incentives will change over time, and what motivates software engineers at their core.

Where the platform actually is

The sessions were grounded in work that already exists rather than a roadmap, so it is worth saying plainly what runs today and what does not.

LLMAO is the gateway described above: metered, attributed model access for Apache projects, at llm.apache.org.

Gofannon is an agent workbench for the Foundation, based on the project of the same name that originated at the AI Alliance. It is where agents get built and run, so that a project can get useful work done without standing up its own infrastructure.

Memory is the newest piece, and the least built. The problem it addresses is that agents start every run empty: a scan run this month has no idea what last month's scan found or what the project already fixed. Memory gives agents somewhere to write down what is worth keeping and somewhere to read what an earlier run learned, with provenance attached so a human can see where a claim came from. It is being built at pilot scope, on the services the platform already runs.

Alongside the platform, Foundation-scale security scanning has continued. A recent pass covered 230 repositories across 75 project management committees in three days. The results of that work are what the memory pilot is being tested against first.

Two adjacent projects came up repeatedly in the hallway track as well: Apache Magpie, agent-assisted infrastructure for maintaining open source repositories, and Apache Sourcelume, instrumentation for tracking the provenance of AI training data.

Coming up: FeatherCast

We recorded with FeatherCast, the ASF's podcast, while we were in Glasgow. The episode is not out yet; we will link it here and on What's New when it lands.
